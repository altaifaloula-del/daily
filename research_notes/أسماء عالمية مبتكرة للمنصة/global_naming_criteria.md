# Global Invented Brand Names for a Saudi B2B SaaS/ERP: Criteria and Evidence (as of Nov 2026)

Scope: criteria to rank invented (coined) names for a Saudi cloud ERP (accounting, POS, inventory, payroll/HR, ZATCA e-invoicing) that must work in Arabic and English. Research method: ~18 web searches/fetches (Nov 25, 2026). Note: www.odoo.com was unreachable from this session (network allowlist), so Odoo's own 2014 rename post was read only via search snippets.

## Q1. What naming criteria do agencies and trademark practitioners recommend for invented global names?

### Takeaway
Agencies (Igor, Lexicon) and trademark practitioners converge on: the name must stand on its own without explanation, sound good (sound symbolism), be distinctive and legally ownable, and be easy to spell/say. Domains are a practical constraint, not a veto: a prefixed .com or alternative TLD is an accepted bridge, and several famous brands renamed precisely because the original name was unpronounceable or un-registrable as a domain.

### Cited Findings
- Igor's naming guide ("Building the Perfect Beast") lists evaluation criteria including Appearance, Distinctiveness, Depth, Energy, Humanity, Positioning, Sound, "33" (word-of-mouth buzz) and trademark viability. — [Igor Naming Guide](https://www.igorinternational.com/process/brand-name-naming-agency-startup.php); [Igor llms.txt summary](https://www.igorinternational.com/llms.txt)
- Igor accepts invented names but warns their rationale cannot rest on convoluted reasoning from languages the audience does not speak; a name must stand on its own without explanation. Foreign-language names are treated as effectively invented. — [Igor Naming Guide](https://www.igorinternational.com/process/brand-naming-expert.php). (Wikipedia characterizes Igor as advising against invented words; the guide's own text is more nuanced — [Wikipedia: Igor Naming Agency](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Igor_Naming_Agency).)
- Lexicon Branding (David Placek; Swiffer, Pentium, BlackBerry, Fios, Febreze, Dasani) grounds naming in "sound symbolism" — tracking associations evoked by specific letters/sounds across languages and cultures; Placek found "b" one of the most reliable letters (influencing BlackBerry). — [The Hustle via Lexicon](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/the-hustle-the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies/); [UCLA Magazine](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/david-placek-lexicon-brand-naming)
- Swiffer was built from sound words ("swipe", "swish", "swiff" + "-er"); Pentium used the "-ium" suffix to suggest a fundamental element. — [Lexicon / The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/originals/the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-art-of-creating-brand-names)
- Placek claims invented names like Pentium can take less money to build into brands than existing words. — [The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/originals/the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies) (agency claim, not independent evidence)
- Paul Graham argues a startup without the matching .com should probably consider renaming. — [paulgraham.com](https://paulgraham.com/name.html)
- Practitioners describe prefixed domains (e.g., getchorus.com, goshippo.com) as a bridge many companies use before buying the bare .com once funded. — [echai.ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/from/buycompanyname-com)
- Alternative TLDs can offer speed to market without closing the door on a later .com; .co is often called the closest substitute but country codes can feel limiting for a global brand. — [Name.com blog](https://www.name.com/blog/why-many-startups-are-choosing-alternate-tlds); [Wix](https://www.wix.com/blog/best-domain-extensions-for-startups)
- Cautionary cost example: one startup reportedly spent $1.8M of a $2.5M raise on friend.com. — [Identity Digital](https://identity.digital/newsroom/the-rise-of-non-traditional-domains-in-tech-startups)

### Inferences — Proposed scoring rubric (100 points) for ranking candidate names
Hard gates (fail = drop the name, regardless of score):
- G1. Not a negative/vulgar/religiously sensitive word or close sound-alike in Arabic (Gulf/Egyptian/Levantine dialects), English, French, Urdu, Hindi, Tagalog, Turkish (key expatriate/user languages in KSA) — native-speaker check.
- G2. No identical/confusingly similar registered or pending mark in Nice classes 9, 35, 36, 42 in SAIP, GCC states, WIPO Madrid, USPTO, EUIPO (knockout search).
- G3. Not descriptive of accounting/ERP/invoicing in either language (would draw a distinctiveness refusal — see Q4).
- G4. At least one acceptable domain path secured (bare .com, or get/try/use-prefix .com, plus .sa and ideally .app/.io) and matching handles obtainable with minor variation.

Weighted criteria:
1. Trademark distinctiveness (fanciful > arbitrary > suggestive) — 20
2. Bilingual pronounceability: uses only sounds that exist in both Arabic and English (avoid P, V, G-ambiguity, "th", hamza, emphatic/ʿayn/ḥā/qāf/khā clusters) and is read the same way by an Arabic and an English speaker on first sight — 15
3. Spelling unambiguity / "radio test" (hear it once, type it right in Latin script; one obvious Arabic spelling) — 10
4. Length/rhythm: ideally 4–6 letters, 2 syllables (max 3); CV-CV structure; open vowel ending — 10
5. Sound symbolism fit for B2B finance: trustworthy/solid (stops like b, d, k, t; vowels a/o) yet modern — 10
6. Arabic-script appearance: short, joins well, no confusable dotted letters, visually balanced in logo — 5
7. Positive or neutral latent associations in Arabic (a subtle Arabic root echo is a bonus, not a requirement — cf. Careem, Qonto) and no limiting product word (cf. ZenPayroll → Gusto) — 10
8. Distinctiveness vs. category peers in KSA (does not sound like Qoyod, Wafeq, Rewaa, Daftra, Zoho, Xero, Odoo, Foodics) — 10
9. Digital availability (domain tier, App Store/Play search collision, social handles, SEO uniqueness: search results for the string are mostly empty) — 10
Score bands: ≥80 shortlist for full legal clearance; 65–79 hold; <65 drop.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Landor or Interbrand published naming criteria pages; the rubric above relies mainly on Igor, Lexicon and practitioner sources.
- No quantitative study found linking name length/syllables to B2B SaaS success; length targets are practitioner convention, not evidence.

## Q2. Which sounds cause problems between Arabic and English, and how did MENA brands handle them?

### Takeaway
Arabic lacks native /p/ and /v/ (speakers substitute /b/ and /f/, e.g., Pepsi → "Bebsi"), and many Arabic consonants (ʿayn, ḥā, qāf, khā, emphatics, hamza) have no stable Latin spelling. Successful MENA brands mostly picked short real Arabic words built from sounds shared with English (Salla, Careem, Tamara, Noon, Hala, Zid) and accepted non-standard Latin spellings (Careem with C, Qoyod with Q, Swvl without vowels) to get a unique, ownable string.

### Cited Findings
- Arabic has no letter "p": Pepsi is "Bebsi" in the Gulf; the same applies to Pringles, Hewlett Packard. — [Yalla magazine / MECS Dubai](https://issuu.com/mecsdubai/docs/yalla_october_22/s/43742009); [ArabicPod101](https://www.arabicpod101.com/blog/2021/05/13/english-loanwords-in-arabic/)
- "V" has no Arabic letter; many speakers pronounce it as "f" ("very" → "ferry"). — [PronunciationPro](https://www.pronunciationpro.com/is-arabic-your-first-language-common-pronunciation-problems-you-may-face/)
- Some Arabic speakers do pronounce /p/ and /v/ in loanwords and write them with adapted letters (پ, ڤ), but inconsistently. — [Language on the Move](https://languageonthemove.com/transliterated-brand-names/)
- A 2020 perception study found Arabic-speaking participants had difficulty perceiving /p/ and /v/, confusing them with /b/ and /f/. — [DergiPark study](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/1480329)
- Careem: name means "generous" (Arabic usually transliterated "kareem"); founders tied it to generosity toward customers/captains; launched during Ramadan (echo of "Ramadan Kareem"). MIT Sloan ME calls it partly "a happy accident" because careem.com was available. — [McKinsey interview](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/careems-co-founders-on-finding-purpose-building-a-unicorn); [The National](https://thenationalnews.com/business/journey-driven-by-generosity-1.292724); [MIT Sloan ME](https://www.mitsloanme.com/?p=13670); [Entrepreneur ME](https://mena.entrepreneur.com/growth-strategies/the-ride-continues-how-careems-culture-fueled-the-careem/348247)
- Salla (سلة) simply means "basket"; Salla says it symbolizes trade and opportunity; founded 2016 in Makkah. — [Entrepreneur ME](https://mena.entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/saudi-unicorns-how-makkah-based-salla-is-helping-merchants/478045); [Salla official info](https://salla.com/ai-info/)
- Swvl is pronounced "swivel" — a vowel-less spelling of an English word; no source explains the spelling choice. — [Wikiwand: Swvl](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Swvl); [The National](https://thenationalnews.com/business/technology/2021/07/29/generation-start-up-who-is-swvls-mostafa-kandil-and-where-is-the-app-going-next)
- Tabby: founded 2019 by Hosam Arab and Daniil Barkalov, now Riyadh-HQ; no source found explaining the name. — [Entrepreneur ME](https://mena.entrepreneur.com/leadership/the-100-hosam-arab-co-founder-and-ceo-tabby/484859); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/08/04/dubai-based-buy-now-pay-later-platform-tabby-raises-50m-at-300m-valuation)
- Zid: Saudi company est. 2017, Riyadh; no source found explaining the name. — [Zid about](https://zid.sa/en/about-zid/)

### Inferences
- Unsourced lexical glosses (standard Arabic dictionary meanings, not founder statements — verify before quoting): Zid ≈ "increase/add more" (زِد); Tamara ≈ "a date (fruit)" (تمرة); Noon = the Arabic letter ن; Hala ≈ "welcome" (هلا); Talabat = "orders" (طلبات); Anghami from نغم "melody"; Qoyod = "entries/constraints" (قيود — in accounting, journal entries); Wafeq ≈ "harmonious/reconciling" (وافق); Rewaa (رواء) ≈ "quenching/freshness"; Tarabut ≈ "interconnection" (ترابط); Fawry ≈ "instant" (فوري). Pattern: most MENA winners are real Arabic words (arbitrary or suggestive marks), not coined words; Qoyod and Talabat are close to descriptive.
- Latin spelling choices: Q for ق (Qoyod, Qonto-style), C for ك (Careem), doubled vowels (Rewaa, Noon) and dropped vowels (Swvl) are used to create unique strings; but each non-obvious spelling costs "radio test" points for English speakers.
- Sound rules for the rubric: prefer b, d, f, h, k, l, m, n, r, s, t, w, y, z and vowels a, i, o/u; avoid p, v, hard-g ambiguity (Egyptian "g" vs Gulf "j" for ج), "th" (ث/ذ inconsistently pronounced across dialects), ch, x (no single Arabic letter), and Arabic-only sounds (ع ح ق خ غ ض ظ ط ص) that English speakers will mispronounce. Start/end on sounds that write cleanly in Arabic without hamza ambiguity (e.g., initial "A" may be أ/ا/ع).
- Note: "Xero" would be awkward in Arabic script (X has no single letter → زيرو), whereas "Odoo" (أودو) and "Zoho" (زوهو) map cleanly — an illustration of the bilingual test.

### Gaps
- No founder sources found for the names Tabby, Tamara, Foodics, Zid, Rewaa, Lean, Hala, Noon, Anghami, Talabat, Fawry, Tarabut, Qoyod or Wafeq within the tool budget; meanings above are lexical, not documented intent.
- No published study on how MENA B2B buyers perceive invented vs. Arabic-word brand names.

## Q3. How did global SaaS/ERP/fintech brands pick their names?

### Takeaway
Most successful global names are short (4–7 letters), 1–3 syllables, and either invented/foreign-derived (Xero, Odoo, Qonto, Zoho) or arbitrary real words (Stripe, Gusto, Ramp, Sage). Several renamed away from descriptive or unpronounceable names (Accounting 2.0 → Xero, OpenERP → Odoo, /dev/payments → Stripe, ZenPayroll → Gusto), which is the strongest evidence for choosing a distinctive, non-limiting name from day one.

### Cited Findings
- Xero was first called "Accounting 2.0"; staff disliked it and the decimal point made a domain impossible. xero.com was previously a Linkin Park fan site (Xero was the band's early name). — [HandWiki: Xero](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Software:Xero); [11:FS interview with Rod Drury](https://11fs.com/blog/rod-drury-xeros-origin-story-new-partnerships-xerocon/) (rationale for "Xero" itself not documented in what I retrieved)
- Odoo: TinyERP (2005) → OpenERP (2008) → Odoo (2014). The company wanted a name it could build each standalone app on, breaking the "one big block" ERP image, and dropped "Open" and "ERP". Its claim that it analysed top internet names and picked three O's was tongue-in-cheek; the company says the name has no meaning. Early user reaction included puzzlement/giggles. — [Odoo blog (2014)](https://www.odoo.com/blog/5/post/156); [Odoo forum](https://www.odoo.com/forum/help-1/what-does-odoo-mean-52443); [Wikipedia: Odoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odoo); [OpenERP community list](https://lists.launchpad.net/openerp-community/msg05708.html)
- Qonto: built from the word for "account" in several European languages (compte, Konto, conto) to create "a name that all European countries could understand." — [Qonto help center](https://help.qonto.com/en/articles/4359675-what-s-the-story-behind-qonto)
- Stripe started as "/dev/payments"; Patrick Collison said the name was "horrendous to say" and slashes are not allowed in company names; "PayDemon" was also considered. — [Morgan Linton](https://www.morganlinton.com/how-stripe-became-stripe-a-look-inside-the-startup-naming-process/); [Startup Grind](https://www.startupgrind.com/blog/the-collison-brothers-and-story-behind-the-founding-of-stripe); [Wikipedia: Stripe](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Stripe,_Inc.)
- Gusto: ZenPayroll was picked days before YC Demo Day 2012; renamed Gusto in Sept 2015 because the old name was too limiting as it expanded beyond payroll into benefits. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/09/17/zenpayroll-is-now-gusto-adds-benefits-to-take-on-zenefits); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2015/09/17/zenpayroll-now-gusto/); [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/this-is-how-you-design-a-lasting-brand-an-inside-look-at-gustos-reinvention/)
- Rippling: a third-party guide says the name turns a single metaphor (changes rippling through all systems) into a product thesis; no founder source retrieved. — [ZenBusiness](https://www.zenbusiness.com/start-a-business/technology/hr-tech-saas/names.html)

### Inferences
- Lesson for a multi-module ERP: avoid product words (ERP, books, pay, POS, fatoora, hisab) in the master brand; they limit expansion (ZenPayroll, OpenERP) and weaken trademark registrability. Module names can be descriptive sub-brands under a distinctive master brand (Odoo's rationale).
- Qonto is the closest analogue to the brief: a coined name derived from a shared root across target languages. An equivalent approach would echo a concept shared by Arabic and English (e.g., balance/weight/count) in a coined form — while respecting Igor's warning that the name must work without the backstory.

### Gaps
- No primary sources retrieved for the origins of Zoho, Sage, Brex, Ramp, Pleo, Deel, Paddle, Rippling (founder-stated). Treat any common explanations (e.g., Zoho from "Zero Hour"? Brex from "breakthrough+?") as unverified; I did not confirm them.

## Q4. Trademark strength spectrum and Saudi/GCC/international registration

### Takeaway
Fanciful (invented) marks sit at the strong end of the distinctiveness spectrum everywhere; descriptive marks are refused in the US and face distinctiveness refusals and oppositions in Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia joined the Madrid Protocol (in force for KSA on 8 Oct 2026), so a Saudi national filing can now serve as the base for a single international application, but KSA uses an 18-month refusal window.

### Cited Findings
- USPTO: fanciful marks are invented words with meaning only in relation to the goods (KODAK, EXXON); arbitrary marks are real words unrelated to the goods; both are "strong"; merely descriptive wording is generally refused on the Principal Register absent acquired distinctiveness. — [USPTO: Strong trademarks](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/strong-trademarks)
- Suggestive marks are inherently distinctive (no secondary meaning needed) but rank below arbitrary/fanciful. — [BitLaw](https://www.bitlaw.com/trademark/degrees.html); [Justia](https://justia.com/intellectual-property/trademarks/strength-of-marks/fanciful-marks)
- A 2026 Berkeley Technology Law Journal article argues fanciful marks draw strength from pure novelty and should not be treated as equal to arbitrary marks. — [BTLJ, Marlan 2026](https://btlj.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Marlan_ReadyforPublication_8-11-2026_ADA-Compliant-1.pdf)
- Saudi Arabia deposited its Madrid Protocol accession instrument on 8 July 2026; in force for KSA on 8 October 2026; Madrid membership now 117; KSA extended the provisional-refusal period to 18 months (opposition-based refusals may come later); KSA charges an individual fee. — [WIPO news](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/madrid-system/w/news/2026/saudi-arabia-joins-wipos-madrid-system); [WIPO Information Notice 35/2026](https://www.wipo.int/documents/d/madrid-system/information-notices-en-2026-madrid_2026_35_e.pdf-1)
- Saudi applicants can use a national filing as the basic Madrid application; local representation may be needed if a designation meets refusal or opposition in KSA. — [DLA Piper (Aug 2026)](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/08/saudi-arabia-accedes-to-the-madrid-protocol)
- Saudi trademark law: Law of Trademarks (Royal Decree M/21) / GCC Trademark Law framework; marks lacking distinctive character are excluded; SAIP may refuse for lack of distinctiveness; one chance to amend or disclaim descriptive elements; accepted marks published for 60-day opposition (including lack of distinctiveness); refusal appealable within 60 days. — [WIPO Lex: Saudi Law of Trademarks](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/206418); [Mondaq comparative guide](https://www.mondaq.com/Article/816058); [Sabaip](https://sabaip.com/?p=21344); [AHYSP via Mondaq](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/saudiarabia/trademark/1711090/how-to-register-a-trademark-in-saudi-arabia-a-legal-guide-for-businesses)
- SAIP changed its procedure for rejected applications in 2024. — [AIPPI](https://www.aippi.org/news/saip-implements-new-rejection-procedure-for-trade-mark-applications/); [AGIP](https://www.agip.com/News.aspx?id=22168)
- Saudi Arabia adopted Nice Classification 12th edition. — [Chambers Doing Business In 2025](https://gpg-pdf.chambers.com/doing-business-in-2025/730)

### Inferences
- An Arabic accounting word (e.g., قيود, ميزان, فاتورة, حساب) used for accounting software is at best suggestive and at risk of descriptive refusal or weak scope in KSA; an invented Latin/Arabic coinage is the safest route to a registrable, enforceable mark across SAIP, GCC, USPTO and EUIPO.
- Because first-to-file is the general rule in KSA/GCC (practitioner consensus; not separately verified in this session), file in KSA in classes 9, 35, 36, 42 (and possibly 41 for training) in both Latin and Arabic-script versions immediately after clearance, then extend via Madrid (US, EU, UK, Egypt, etc.) within the 6-month Paris priority window. GCC states not in Madrid need national filings (status per state should be checked).
- Register both the Latin word mark and the Arabic-script transliteration; in Arabic examination, a transliteration that happens to be a real Arabic descriptive word could trigger a distinctiveness objection even if the Latin form is coined.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the exact current Saudi statutory article text on first-to-file or the status of the unified GCC trademark law across all six states; verify with SAIP or local counsel.
- Saudi individual Madrid fee amounts were to be announced in a separate WIPO notice; not retrieved.

## Q5. Practical clearance workflow for a global name

### Takeaway
A defensible workflow runs from cheap, fast screens (linguistic, search engine, domains, app stores) to formal trademark knockout searches across WIPO Global Brand Database, TMview, USPTO and SAIP, then a full counsel search for the top 2–3 names, then immediate filing.

### Cited Findings
- WIPO's Madrid system now covers KSA (from 8 Oct 2026), enabling one international application from a Saudi base filing. — [WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/madrid-system/w/news/2026/saudi-arabia-joins-wipos-madrid-system)
- Lexicon screens names linguistically across languages/cultures as part of its method. — [Lexicon / The Hustle](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/the-hustle-the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies/)
- Domain fallbacks (prefix .com, alt TLD) are standard practice and can be upgraded later. — [echai.ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/from/buycompanyname-com); [Name.com](https://www.name.com/blog/why-many-startups-are-choosing-alternate-tlds)

### Inferences — Recommended workflow (ordered)
1. Longlist (50–200) → apply hard gates G1/G3 internally.
2. Search-engine and app-store screen (Google, App Store, Google Play, Product Hunt, Crunchbase, LinkedIn companies): drop names already used by software/fintech.
3. Domain/handle screen: bare .com (or price of aftermarket), get/try/use + name .com, .sa (via SaudiNIC), .app, .io; handles on X, LinkedIn, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok.
4. Knockout trademark search (identical + phonetic variants, both scripts) in WIPO Global Brand Database, EUIPO TMview, USPTO search, SAIP e-services, and UAE/Egypt registers; classes 9, 35, 36, 42.
5. Linguistic screening by native speakers: Gulf, Egyptian, Levantine Arabic; English (US/UK); French; Urdu/Hindi; Tagalog; Turkish; Bahasa — meaning, slang, sound-alikes, pronunciation consistency, Arabic-script rendering.
6. Customer perception test (5–10 target SME owners/accountants in KSA): pronounce-after-reading, spell-after-hearing, trust/fit rating.
7. Full search + opinion by IP counsel for top 2–3 names.
8. File SAIP (Latin + Arabic script) immediately; secure domains/handles same day; Madrid extension within 6 months.

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve official pages for WIPO Global Brand Database, TMview or SAIP search tool features; workflow step details are based on general practice and should be confirmed.
- No source found on SaudiNIC .sa eligibility rules for foreign-registered brands (not researched).
